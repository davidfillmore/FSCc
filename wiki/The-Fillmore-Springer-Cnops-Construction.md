"FSCc" abbreviates *Fillmore–Springer–Cnops construction*, Kisil's name for the
linearisation of inversive geometry that goes back to Schwerdtfeger and was set
up over general fields by Fillmore and Springer; the lowercase *c* is
"construction". This page states the language in the form used on the other
pages.

## 1. Cycles as vectors

Nineteenth-century *inversive geometry* studies circles and lines in the plane
(collectively, *cycles*) under the transformations generated by inversions, the
Möbius transformations. Its founding trick is linearisation: the circle

```math
k(x^2+y^2)-2\ell_1x-2\ell_2y+m=0
```

is encoded by the vector $`(k,\ell_1,\ell_2,m)`$, defined up to overall scale;
lines are the cycles with $`k=0`$; and the geometry (tangency, orthogonality,
inversion) becomes linear algebra on these vectors, equipped with an invariant
quadratic form of Minkowski type. J. P. Fillmore and A. Springer [FS] set this
up over an arbitrary field of characteristic $`\neq2`$ with an arbitrary
nondegenerate quadratic form: spheres in a quadratic space $`X`$ become vectors
in a *cycle space* of dimension $`\dim X+2`$ carrying the invariant symmetric
form $`w\cdot w-w^0w^\infty`$, with the Möbius group acting linearly. Cnops
[Cnops] gives the Clifford-algebra formulation; Kisil [Kisil 2010, Kisil 2019]
gives the matrix form used below and extends the construction in two
directions described in §5.

## 2. The power of a point

The classical invariant of a point and a circle is the *power of the point*: for
a circle of centre $`O`$ and radius $`r`$ and a point $`P`$,

```math
\mathrm{pow}(P)=|PO|^2-r^2,
```

equivalently the product of signed distances from $`P`$ to the two intersection
points along any secant. It vanishes exactly when the point lies on the circle,
it is positive outside and negative inside, and it is the quantity that
inversion in the circle preserves up to scale. Everything on the
[dictionary page](The-Inversive-Dictionary.md) turns on the fact that the
*resultant* of a linear and a quadratic binary form is this classical quantity
(Figure 1 there draws it).

## 3. The one-dimensional case: cycles on a line are point-pairs

Only the smallest case is needed: cycles on a *line*. There a cycle

```math
ku^2-2lu+m=0
```

is a point-pair, its two roots, that is, exactly a binary quadratic. With the
monic normalisation $`k=1`$ and roots $`a,b`$ the power of the point $`u`$ is

```math
\mathrm{pow}(u)=(u-a)(u-b),
```

*the value of the quadratic at the point*. It vanishes exactly when the point
lies on the cycle. In the coefficient convention of the other pages,
$`Q(u,1)=cu^2+du+e`$ for the binary quadratic $`Q=cs^2+dst+et^2`$, the
identification is

```math
(k,l,m)=(c,\,-\tfrac d2,\,e).
```

On the real coefficient form the three-dimensional cycle space carries an
invariant quadratic form of signature $`(2,1)`$, a small Minkowski space. After
complexification there is no signature, only a nondegenerate complex quadratic
form; the next section realises the complexified space as trace-free
$`2\times2`$ matrices. Cycles of zero radius are the null vectors: point-pairs
whose two points coincide, sitting on the cone $`\mathrm{disc}=0`$. The
Fillmore–Springer form $`w\cdot w-w^0w^\infty`$ is the discriminant up to
normalisation.

## 4. Kisil's cycle matrices

Kisil's normalisation [Kisil 2010, Def. 3.1] encodes the cycle $`ku^2-2lu+m=0`$
by the trace-free matrix

```math
C=\begin{pmatrix} l\,i & m\\ k & -l\,i\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\langle C_1,C_2\rangle:=\mathrm{tr}(C_1C_2),\qquad \det C=l^2-km ,
```

on which $`\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})`$, and after complexification
$`\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})`$, acts by conjugation [Kisil 2010, Prop. 3.3].
(Kisil writes a formal hypercomplex unit $`\breve e_0`$ with
$`\breve e_0^2=-1`$ on the diagonal; it is modelled here by the imaginary unit,
which is all that is needed, and it cancels from every trace identity below,
whose values stay in the coefficient field.) In the substitution convention of
these pages the conjugating matrix is an embedded copy $`\rho(g)=P^{-1}gP`$ of
$`g`$, made explicit on the [dictionary page](The-Inversive-Dictionary.md).

Three consequences of the definition.

**Radius.** $`\det C=l^2-km`$ is the squared radius, up to the model constant;
for $`C_Q`$ it equals $`\tfrac14\mathrm{disc}\,Q`$.

**Zero-radius cycles are points.** The cycles with $`\det C=0`$ encode points
equivariantly [Kisil 2010, Def. 3.12, Lemma 3.14]. Writing the cycle of
$`(u-u_0)^2`$, that is $`(k,l,m)=(1,u_0,u_0^2)`$,

```math
Z(u_0)=\begin{pmatrix} i\,u_0 & u_0^2\\ 1 & -i\,u_0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det Z(u_0)=0,
```

a rank-one nilpotent matrix encoding the point $`u_0`$.

**Incidence is a trace.** Pairing a cycle against a point-cycle evaluates the
quadratic at the point: $`\mathrm{tr}\bigl(C_Q\,Z(u_0)\bigr)=Q(u_0,1)`$, which
vanishes exactly when the point lies on the cycle [Kisil 2010, §4]. This is
the power of the point, computed as an invariant trace.

The general trace pairing is, entry by entry,
$`\mathrm{tr}(C_1C_2)=-2l_1l_2+m_1k_2+k_1m_2`$; the imaginary units cancel.

## 5. Kisil's extension: elliptic, parabolic, hyperbolic

Kisil's *Erlangen programme at large* [Kisil 2010, Kisil 2012, Kisil 2019]
decouples the point-space and cycle-space signatures, so that the same matrix
calculus carries three geometries, named after the three conjugacy classes of
one-parameter subgroups of $`\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})`$:

| Class | Subgroup | Fixed points on the projective line | Lorentz name |
|---|---|---|---|
| elliptic | $`K`$, rotations | a conjugate pair, none real | rotation |
| parabolic | $`N`$, unipotent | one | null rotation |
| hyperbolic | $`A`$, the diagonal torus | two distinct real points | boost |

The [stabilizer-torus page](The-Stabilizer-Torus-and-the-EPH-Trichotomy.md) draws
the three pencils (Figure 2) and shows that the grading of the counterexample
is a subgroup of the third kind, and why it could not have been of the first. In a second sense [Kisil 2018] the
construction passes from single cycles to *ensembles* of cycles; that extension
is not used here.

## 6. What the construction contains, and what it does not

Under conjugation the space of $`2\times2`$ matrices is
$`M_2=\mathbb{C}I\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_2\cong\mathrm{Sym}^0\oplus\mathrm{Sym}^2`$,
and it is closed under sums and products. So any polynomial expression in cycle
matrices stays in $`\mathrm{Sym}^2\oplus\mathrm{Sym}^0`$, and its traces and
determinants are invariants: Kisil's general invariant machinery
[Kisil 2010, eq. (4.1)] lives entirely there. The Fillmore–Springer *spinors* do
carry $`\mathrm{Sym}^1`$ [FS]. What no cycle-type formalism contains is the
binary *cubic* $`\mathrm{Sym}^3`$, the target of the counterexample's
multiplication map: the [Möbius page](The-Mobius-Group-and-Binary-Forms.md) proves
that $`\mathrm{Sym}^3`$ carries no invariant symmetric bilinear form at all,
while every cycle space carries one by construction. The dictionary is
therefore intrinsically partial: it covers the source of the counterexample
exactly, and stops at the target.
